# Releases

## [Unreleased]

### What's new
Welcome to Project Arcadia! The game now opens on a brand-new home screen: an astronaut gazes at the Earth, the Moon and Mars. Press « Start playing » to choose your act. The Moon is open as a demo, while Earth and Mars are still locked for now. Pick the Moon and watch your landing: your first step on the dusty ground, a look back at the Earth shining in the black sky, then you turn around and discover your base. Short on time? Press « Skip ». Then comes Day One: the title rises over the base and its first readings appear, the temperature outside, the sunlight and how your crew is doing. Then read your daily briefing: today's goals, your oxygen, power, water and regolith (the dust and broken rock that covers the Moon), and launch the mission when you are ready.

New on the home screen: an « Information » button tells you the story of the mission, what you have to do, how to play, which keys to use and who made the game.

A new "Mission training" page, reachable from the home page, offers 16 mini-games to train like an astronaut: crack the airlock code, test your reflexes, find your way through the station, sort fuel samples, cool down the reactor, repeat the signal lights, restore power, dock the shuttle, follow the co-pilot's directions, switch off the lights, bring water to the greenhouse, find lost tools, remember what you saw through the porthole, balance the cargo arm, aim the laser, and answer a space quiz. Each game ends with a score out of 100.

A new "Planet walk" page, reachable from the home page, lets you walk an astronaut in a spacesuit on the Moon, Mercury, Mars, Venus or the Earth, seen from behind or through its eyes (press V to switch). Walk with Z and S (hold Shift to run), turn with Q and D (W A S D on a QWERTY keyboard) or the arrows, drag the view to look around, and press Space to jump: on the Moon, the same jump takes you six times higher and keeps you almost four seconds in the air! Just ahead of you stands a space base to explore, with its living modules, lab, greenhouse, solar panels, antenna and landing pad. Buildings and rocks are solid: you bump into them, climb onto small rocks, and on the Moon you can even jump onto the base's lower units and walk on their roofs. The base mixes NASA's plans for the Moon and Mars: step inside its cross-shaped habitat, sixteen rooms around the control center, from the kitchen and the gym to the labs, the infirmary, the workshops and the greenhouse, each named by a sign above its door, to see how four astronauts would live and work, then go and look at the lander standing on its pad, the inflatable habitat on legs, the rover, the cargo containers, and the digging robot that scoops up the soil to make water, oxygen and building materials. You go in and out through the airlock, between two doors that stay shut to keep the air inside: stand in front of a door and press E to go through. In the middle of the base, press E at the terminal to open the station dashboard: oxygen, food, water, solar power, battery, radiation outside, date, time and outside temperature, all following the real conditions of the world you chose, from the Moon's long scorching days and freezing nights to the heat of Venus. From there, drive the digging robot yourself: Z and S to move, Q and D to turn, Space to dig in the grey zone (it gathers 1 kg of soil a second, up to 80 kg, and brings in water from icy soil), then drive to the orange drop-off zone by the workshops and press Space to unload, and E to hand it back. Watch its battery at the top of the screen: it starts at 30 %, rolling, digging and unloading drain it, and when it is empty the robot stops for good. Beware: once the robot has loaded 17 kg, a magnetic storm breaks out and scrambles its Z, Q, S and D keys for a minute, changing them again every 15 seconds. Find out which key does what to keep driving!

On a phone or tablet, buttons now appear on the screen: arrows on the left to walk and turn, and on the right E to act, Jump, Run and View.

The facts panel now has a close button, and on phones it opens at the bottom of the screen without covering the other buttons.

Follow the great space probes on their real paths: Voyager 1 and 2 on their grand tour of the giant planets and beyond, Cassini circling Saturn for 13 years, New Horizons flying to Pluto, Curiosity on its way to Mars, and the James Webb telescope heading to its lookout point far beyond the Moon. Twelve new moments in the Events list take you straight to their launches and visits, and each probe's "i" button tells you how far away it is and how long its radio signal takes to reach us.

Relive Apollo 11: pick "Apollo 11 lifts off" in the Events list, then press Play to watch the spacecraft circle the Earth, fly to the Moon, go around it and come back to splash down in the Pacific Ocean. On "First steps on the Moon", you can see it circling the Moon while the astronauts walk on it.

Space history now comes to life: pick "Sputnik 1" or "First human in space" in the Events list to follow the very first satellite, or Yuri Gagarin's capsule on its single trip around the Earth, along a dotted orange path. Press their "i" button to learn how high they fly and how long ago they took off.

Zoom in on the Earth to discover ten famous satellites circling it: the International Space Station, the Hubble telescope, China's Tiangong station, GPS and Galileo satellites that guide your phone, and satellites watching the weather, forests and oceans. Click a name to follow it, and press its "i" button to see how high it flies, how fast, and how many times a day it goes around the Earth.

The site now opens on a deep-space backdrop with drifting stars, a realistic Earth slowly turning on itself, with its clouds, the glow of its atmosphere and city lights shining on the night side, that you can spin and zoom into with your mouse or fingers.

On the left, use the arrows below the planet to travel through the solar system: the Sun, every planet from Mercury to Neptune (Saturn with its rings) and the Moon. In the middle, a second set of arrows lets you browse eight spacecraft: communication, GPS, weather and radar satellites, a space telescope, a space station, a tiny CubeSat and a deep-space probe. On the right, take a closer look at the mission equipment: solar panel, battery, oxygen tanks, electrolyzer, food rations, greenhouse, water tank, science lab, relay antenna and habitat module.

A new "Solar system" page, reachable from the top-right corner of the home page, shows the Sun and the eight planets travelling around it at their real speeds compared to each other: watch Mercury race ahead while Neptune barely moves, and the Moon circle the Earth about once a month, always showing it the same face. Every planet also turns on itself at its true speed, whatever speed you pick for time: the Earth makes one full turn for each day that goes by, and its sunlit side always matches the time of day. When a planet spins too fast for the eye, it turns into a blur of colored stripes, just as a spinning top does. Press Pause to stop time and Play to start it again, or Now to see exactly where each planet is right now. Below, choose how fast time flows: from real time, to watch the Earth slowly turn, up to one year per second, to see the planets race around the Sun.

Click the name of any planet, the Sun or the Moon to fly right up to it and follow it on its journey; the Overview button takes you back to the whole solar system. You can also travel through time: press Rewind to make time flow backwards, or type any date between 1800 and 2050. Try 8 April 2024 at 20:17 Paris time to see the Moon's shadow sweep over Mexico during a total solar eclipse, or 8 November 2022 at 11:59 Paris time to watch the Moon turn red in the Earth's shadow.

The planets always travel on their true orbits: Neptune really is thirty times farther from the Sun than the Earth. To make them easy to see, they are drawn much bigger than they really are. Press Real scale at the top left to see their true size: the planets shrink to tiny dots lost in space, and the Moon moves out to circle the Earth thirty Earths away. Press Easy view to go back.

Want to know more about a planet? Press the little "i" button next to its name. You will see how big it is next to the Earth, how long its days and years last, and how far it is from the Sun and from us right now: how long light takes to get there, and how many years a car would need to drive there! Lines appear in space to show these distances.

Not sure where to start? Open the Events list at the top left and pick a moment: the first steps on the Moon, Venus crossing the Sun, Jupiter and Saturn side by side, the last eclipses and the next ones. You are taken straight to the right date, looking at the right place.

Behind the planets, the sky is now the real one: the nearly 9,000 stars you can see with the naked eye, in their true places and colors, turning with you as you move around. Look for the constellations of the zodiac along the planets' path.

The site now has its own icon in browser tabs, and links shared on social networks or messaging apps display a preview card with the page title and visual.
